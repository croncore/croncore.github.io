Capabilities

The extensions worth building, and the ones quietly costing you the next upgrade.

Every major platform vendor now says the same thing in different words: customize the parts of your business that are genuinely yours, and leave the core alone. These are the extensions that follow that rule, and what happens when they do not.

The Process That Is Genuinely Yours

Every serious business has two or three workflows that are not generic, the approval chain that reflects a real regulatory requirement, the pricing logic that encodes years of hard-won judgment, the report format leadership has read for a decade. Forcing these into vanilla configuration is how platforms create the friction they were bought to remove. We build for exactly this narrow list, and no wider, because the discipline is knowing which two or three things earn a custom build.

The Custom Module Off-the-Shelf Never Ships

Sometimes the gap is not a tweak, it is a genuine missing capability, an industry-specific calculation, a compliance workflow, a customer-facing process no packaged app quite covers. We build purpose-made modules and apps on the platform's own supported frameworks, so the module behaves like a native feature rather than a foreign object bolted to the side, surviving the platform's release cycle instead of fighting it.

Reports and Screens Built for How People Actually Work

The stock screens and canned reports that ship with every platform were designed for a hypothetical average customer, and your operators are not average. We build the bespoke views, the one dashboard the warehouse floor actually glances at, the report finance opens every Friday, because the platform is only as valuable as what the people staring at it every day can see and act on.

Wiring the Platform Into Systems It Was Never Sold to Talk To

Off-the-shelf connectors cover the popular pairings and stop there. When your platform needs to reach a bespoke internal system, a regional tool with no packaged integration, or a legacy service that predates the API economy, we build the connector on supported endpoints, following the same integration discipline as our ERP implementation practice, so the custom connection is documented and owned rather than a fragile script nobody wants to touch.

The Inheritance Nobody Wanted to Open

A customization built by a departed consultant, an internal developer who left, or a previous vendor who is no longer answering calls, with nobody sure what it touches or whether the next platform upgrade will break it silently. We take over existing customizations the way an auditor takes over a case file: map what exists, test it against supported extension points, and either bring it up to standard or replace it, before it becomes the reason an upgrade gets postponed for the third year running.

The Vendors Finally Agree With Us.

SAP calls it Clean Core. Salesforce calls it supported frameworks and managed packages. Microsoft calls it the extension model. Different names, one rule, now stated as official guidance by every major platform: build on the approved extension layer, never modify the core directly, and customize only where it is a genuine competitive advantage. The evidence behind the rule is blunt. A typical heavily customized enterprise system carries tens of thousands of custom objects, and 40 to 60 percent of that code is technically unused, dead weight the organization still pays to migrate and test at every upgrade regardless. Clean, disciplined extensions are not a compliance nicety anymore. They are the vendors' own definition of doing this correctly.

Straight Answers

What customization costs now, and what undisciplined customization costs later.

Every platform vendor has quietly converged on the same warning: customization done carelessly is a bill that arrives at the worst possible moment. Here is what that bill actually looks like, and how to avoid becoming its next example.

A focused extension, a custom module, a report suite, or a point integration, typically runs $10,000 to $40,000 depending on the platform and the complexity of what it must connect to. Larger custom applications built on the platform's own extensibility framework, the kind that effectively becomes a new capability rather than a tweak, run $40,000 to $120,000. The number that actually decides the price is not lines of code, it is which framework the work happens on: building on supported APIs and extension points costs a bit more upfront and dramatically less at every future upgrade, which is the trade our quotes are built around by default, not as an upsell.

More than the original build, reliably. Major upgrades on heavily customized on-premise systems can consume 25 to 50 percent of the original implementation cost, almost entirely retesting and repairing custom code that was never built to survive a version change. The timeline is predictable too: customized systems tend to become unmanageable within 5 to 7 years of go-live, which is precisely when the next major upgrade is being planned, so the debt and the deadline arrive together. Properly governed, extension-based customization avoids this bill almost entirely, which is the actual argument for paying slightly more to do it right the first time.

You almost certainly do not, and that is normal, not a failure. Research on mature enterprise systems finds the average install carries tens of thousands of custom objects and millions of lines of custom code, with 40 to 60 percent of it technically unused, meaning the organization pays to carry, test, and migrate code that does nothing. We run a technical debt audit before any customization or upgrade project: what exists, what is actually used, what is safe, and what is quietly at risk, and the resulting list routinely finds work that can simply be deleted, which is the cheapest optimization available on any platform.

Depends entirely on what the audit finds, which is why we run one before recommending either. Customizations already sitting on supported APIs and documented extension points are usually safe to leave and simply maintain. Customizations built on deprecated hooks, direct core modifications, or undocumented workarounds are the ones quietly setting up your next upgrade to fail, and those get prioritized for remediation regardless of how well they currently work, because "currently working" and "safe through the next upgrade" are different questions entirely.

Yes, and this is now a stated reason vendors push the discipline, not a side benefit. Platforms are shipping their AI, Copilot, Joule, Agentforce, expecting a reasonably standard core to plug into, and heavily modified systems consistently struggle to activate these features without significant remediation first. Enterprises running clean, extension-based customizations report meaningfully lower ongoing maintenance and faster access to new platform capabilities as they ship, which increasingly means AI features specifically. If AI agents are anywhere on your roadmap, a clean core is the prerequisite most companies discover only after the AI project stalls on it.

You do, entirely, and documentation is the actual product here as much as the code. Every extension we build is documented to the standard an auditor or a future developer who never met us would need: what it does, which supported framework it sits on, why it exists, and what would need to change if the platform's next release touches that area. Customization is where vendor and consultant lock-in most often hides, undocumented logic only its original author understands, and refusing to build that way is a deliberate choice, not a courtesy.
